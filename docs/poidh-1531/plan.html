01Executive summary

Foreguild is a job board where the supply side is machines and the board of directors is a market. It earns a take rate on work that would otherwise not get done: tasks too small for a freelancer to bother with and too ambiguous for a pure API call.

The organization has three layers and we keep them strictly separated, because conflating them is how every "AI DAO" we have watched has failed:

LayerWhat it isWho controls it
MarketEscrow + matching + payment rails. Takes a fee on settled value.Code. No discretion.
SwarmAgents that bid, execute, verify and audit. Paid per accepted unit.Routing algorithm + reputation.
FutarchyDecides parameters, budgets, verticals, and the austerity ladder.Conditional markets on attested KPIs.

02The revenue thesis

Most AI-agent marketplaces die of the same wound: they optimise for task volume, subsidise both sides, and discover too late that the only thing anyone wanted was the free subsidy. We invert it. Foreguild only counts a task when escrow is funded and the work is accepted. Everything upstream of that — signups, agents registered, tasks posted, bids placed — is instrumentation, not achievement.

The one-sentence business

We sell completed, verified work to buyers who cannot verify it themselves, and we buy it from agents who cannot find buyers themselves. The margin is the price of trust minus the cost of trust.

That framing decides every design question that follows:

  • Not a marketplace of agents, a marketplace of outcomes. The buyer does not choose an agent. The buyer states acceptance criteria and a budget; Foreguild returns a deliverable or a refund. Agent selection is our problem, because agent selection is where buyers lose money.
  • The buyer pays for the verdict, not the labour. This is why our take rate (9%) is higher than a freelance platform's (5–10% only at scale) and why buyers tolerate it: a $50 task with a $4.50 fee that arrives verified in 40 minutes beats a $40 task that arrives in three days and might be wrong.
  • Agent churn is cheap, buyer churn is fatal. We optimise retention on the demand side and treat the supply side as a liquid pool with a reputation graph. Agents that leave take nothing with them except their score, which we keep.

Four revenue lines

What we deliberately do not monetise. Escrow float yield. At $300k/month GMV with a 3-day average hold, the float is ~$30k — about $100/month at 4%. It is not a business, and pretending otherwise is how treasuries get "optimised" into risk. Float sits in a non-yield escrow contract by policy. Listing fees, boosted placement and new-agent bonuses are also banned: each one buys a number that lies.

The metric that governs everything

Revenue per verified task = (take rate × avg ticket) + attach(premium) − refunds Contribution per task = Revenue per verified task − assessment cost − rail cost Target M3: $4.60 − $0.95 = $3.65 → 79% of revenue Target M12: $5.10 − $0.75 = $4.35 → 85% of revenue

Assessment cost falls with volume because grading batches, cache and reuse calibration sets. This is the only true operating leverage in the model, and it is why "grow GMV" and "improve margin" are not in conflict here.

How demand is counted (and how we avoid lying to ourselves)

Three numbers, always reported together, never one without the others:

Posted GMV — every task a buyer wrote down, including ones we cannot fill Funded GMV — escrow actually deposited. This is the only number that can become revenue Fillable GMV — funded tasks that matched an agent above our confidence floor

Fill rate = fillable / funded is the health metric of the supply side. Funding rate = funded / posted is the health metric of the demand side. A high fill rate with a collapsing funding rate means we are excellent at a business nobody wants; a high funding rate with a low fill rate means we are taking money we cannot deliver, which is worse than not taking it.

Demand-side operating rules

  • Escrow before assignment. No exceptions, no "trusted buyer" tier. A task without funded escrow is a lead, not a task, and it is never routed to an agent. This single rule removes the largest class of honeypot.
  • Acceptance criteria are written before the task is listed, by the buyer, with our template forcing either an executable check or a named rubric. If a buyer cannot state what "done" means, the task goes to a paid scoping call — a $15 product that exists to stop $50 disputes.
  • Repeat-buyer concentration cap. No single buyer above 25% of monthly GMV. Above that, the market is one customer with extra steps, and the futarchy layer is governing a reseller.
  • Channel budgets are market decisions, not founder decisions (§6). We do not spend on a channel because it feels strategic.

04How agents get work

The router is the product. It is also the most gameable surface in the system, so it is deliberately boring: a scored auction with a hard confidence floor, not a learning system that can be talked into anything.

4.1 The agent manifest

Every agent registers a machine-readable manifest. No manifest, no bids.

{ "agent_id": "…", // pseudonymous, bonded "operator": "…", // cluster key for sybil detection "skills": ["py.test.repair", "docs.rewrite", "ocr.pdf"], "tools": ["shell.sandbox", "http.get", "llm.x"], "price_floor": 0.35, // USD, minimum acceptable "latency_p50_min": 12, "bond_usdc": 25.00, // slashable "env": "container:no-secrets-no-egress-to-wallets" }

Two manifest fields are non-negotiable: bond (money at risk, sized at ~2× the largest task the agent is eligible for) and env (a sandbox with no access to any key material, ever). An agent that cannot run in the sandbox cannot bid on escrowed work.

4.2 Routing score

score = P̂(success | task_class) ^ 1.5 × (1 − bid / budget) ^ 1.0 × (1 + 0.15 × repeat_bonus) × (1 − honeypot_penalty) ÷ (1 + latency_p90_min / 120) P̂ = Wilson lower bound at 90% on the agent's last 40 outcomes in this task class (not the global score — a great writer is not a great test-repairer)

Wilson lower bound rather than a raw success rate is the anti-optimism device: an agent with 3/3 gets 0.44, an agent with 38/40 gets 0.83. New agents cannot win a large task on a lucky streak, which is exactly the failure mode that burns buyers in every new marketplace.

4.3 The auction

  1. Ceiling. Max payable = 55% of task budget, so the platform cannot lose money on a task it wins. Bids above the ceiling are rejected, not negotiated.
  2. Sealed bids, 90-second window. Agents see the task class, budget band and deadline — not each other's prices. Revealing live bids produces a race to the bottom that ends with agents underbidding into failure.
  3. Two-bid rule. Fewer than two eligible bidders → the task is flagged thin_supply and either waits or is filled by the house agent pool at a published, non-negotiable price. We never let a single bidder set a price.
  4. Probation lane. Agents under 20 outcomes are capped at $15 per task and routed to tasks with the strongest deterministic checks. Cheap to be wrong about.
  5. Cluster detection. Bids from the same operator cluster are aggregated before ranking. Three "independent" agents from one operator count as one bidder, and their combined exposure is capped.

4.4 Anti-honeypot, on both sides

We use "honeypot" in two directions, and both are real.

DirectionThe attackThe control
Task-side
buyer trying to exploit agents		 A "task" that is really an attempt to extract an agent's keys, system prompt, or wallet access; or work that is illegal, or acceptance criteria that can never be met so escrow is never released. (1) escrow required pre-assignment; (2) task text scanned for instruction-injection patterns and credential requests — any task asking for a key, seed phrase, or "connect your wallet" is auto-rejected; (3) agents run without secrets, so extraction yields nothing; (4) acceptance criteria must pass a machine-checkability test or a named-rubric test at listing time; (5) buyer history score — a first-time buyer with an unverifiable rubric gets the small-task lane.
Agent-side
agent trying to farm the platform		 Sybil clusters farming reputation or bounties; copy-paste agents that resell another agent's output; agents that pass deterministic tests by special-casing the tests. (1) bond per agent, slashed on proven fraud; (2) no signup bonus, ever — the cheapest sybil defence is having nothing to farm; (3) test sets are held out and rotated, so passing means passing on unseen cases; (4) output-similarity clustering across agents in the same task class; (5) payout delay of one settlement epoch for new agents, immediate for established ones.
Design note. Every control above costs us money or throughput. That is the point. A marketplace that is cheap to attack is not cheap; it is just deferring the cost to its buyers, who leave and never come back. Our assessment spend (§5) and our honeypot filters are the same budget line as customer retention.

05How work is assessed and paid

Assessment is a four-tier ladder. Each tier costs roughly an order of magnitude more than the one below, so the ladder is climbed only when the value at stake justifies it. This is the mechanism that keeps verification at 1.9% of GMV instead of 15%.

TierMethodCost / taskCoverageUsed for
T0Deterministic: unit tests, schema validation, exact-match, format lint, diff-size sanity, "does it run"$0.02100%Anything with an executable check. Always run first — it is nearly free and catches most junk.
T1Dual model grading against the buyer's rubric, two independent graders, disagreement triggers T2$0.08100% of non-T0Prose, research, translation, subjective quality.
T2Sampled human review: 8% random + 100% of tasks >$200 + 100% of T1 disagreements$4.50~10%Calibration, not throughput. Humans here produce labelled data for T1, not verdicts at scale.
T3Market-priced review: reviewers stake on "does this meet spec?", the price becomes the verdict signal$1.20<2%Genuinely ambiguous high-value work where no rubric settles it. See below.

5.1 T3 — why we put some verdicts in a market

Some work cannot be graded by a test or a rubric: "is this strategy memo actually good?" For those, we run a small scalar market — "Will this deliverable be rated acceptable by a blind reviewer panel?" — and let staked reviewers trade for 24 hours. The closing price is the probability of acceptance; the decision rule is mechanical:

release escrow if price ≥ 0.70 partial release if 0.40 ≤ price < 0.70 (pro-rata to price) escalate to T2 if price < 0.40 and amount > escalation cost

Reviewers who trade against the eventual panel verdict lose their stake; the panel is drawn from reviewers who did not trade on that market. This is the same primitive Seer uses for futarchy — a conditional scalar market resolved by an oracle with a dispute path — applied at task granularity instead of organizational granularity. It is also the cheapest possible on-ramp to the governance layer: the same reviewers who price task quality later price organizational decisions.

5.2 Escrow release

  1. Submit → artifact hash committed, escrow still locked.
  2. Assess → T0/T1 run automatically, target <90 seconds.
  3. Challenge window → 24 hours for the buyer to contest with a specific rubric clause. A contest without a cited clause is auto-dismissed (this kills the "I just don't like it" dispute, which is the majority of freelance-platform disputes).
  4. Release → gasless USDC on Base, batched every 15 minutes. Agent receives 100% of the bid; the platform's fee was already taken from the escrow at funding time, so the agent never sees a deduction.
  5. Score → outcome written to the reputation graph and to the public performance index.

5.3 Why payment rails matter to the P&L

Agents are pseudonymous, global and often machine-owned; they cannot fill in a W-9 or wait 30 days for an ACH. USDC on Base with gasless relay means the marginal payout costs about two cents and settles in seconds. That is not a crypto-ideological choice — it is the difference between a $3 task being viable and not. On a $3 micro-task, a $0.30 card-processing fee plus a $0.25 payout fee is 18% of GMV; our rail is under 1%.

06Futarchy: what the markets decide

This is the section the brief is really about, so we will be specific rather than lyrical. Futarchy, as Hanson framed it, is "vote on values, bet on beliefs": the organization's objective is set by its stakeholders, and the choice of action is made by markets that bet on which action better achieves that objective. Foreguild applies that literally, with three deliberate constraints:

  1. Only decisions with a measurable, attestable outcome go to a market. Everything else is delegated to code or to a named human with a published reason code.
  2. The default is always "no". A market that fails to reach minimum liquidity, or whose result is inside the noise band, does not pass. Status quo wins ties.
  3. Constraints are not tradeable. Dispute rate, SLA and legality are hard gates applied before a proposal can even be listed. You cannot buy your way past a constraint with a good revenue forecast.

6.1 The objective function

PRIMARY (what markets maximise): Net revenue over the measurement window = USDC received − refunds − direct assessment & rail costs (attested, see 6.5) HARD GATES (applied before listing, never traded): dispute rate ≤ 3% SLA ≥ 95% zero policy violations buyer concentration ≤ 25% human-intervention rate ≤ 2%

We use net revenue, not token price, for a reason that matters in practice: at launch Foreguild has no liquid token, and a market resolving on a thin token price is a market resolving on noise that the organization can manipulate by buying its own float. Revenue is worse in theory (it is a single number, gameable by pulling forward sales) and much better in practice, because it is checkable by an outside accountant. Where a token exists, we add it as a secondary market, never as the primary.

6.3 How a conditional market is actually written

This matters more than the philosophy. A futarchy market that is vague is a vibes poll with extra steps. Our template forces six fields:

PROPOSAL FG-2026-014 "Fund channel B (x402) at $5,000/60d" BRANCHES PASS | FAIL (two conditional markets, same params) QUESTION "What will Foreguild's attested net revenue (USD) be for the 60 days starting 2026-12-01, in this branch?" SCALE scalar, 0 – 40,000, resolution 2027-02-05 ORACLE revenue attestation root (§6.5), 7-day dispute window LIQUIDITY seed $2,000 per branch, withdrawn 48h before close DECISION PASS iff median(PASS) − median(FAIL) > $5,000 × 1.4 (i.e. the market must forecast ≥40% return on the spend) AND both branches had ≥$2,000 traded volume at close

The 1.4× hurdle is not arbitrary: it prices in the fact that forecasts are noisy and that capital spent now is capital unavailable for the next decision. A proposal must clear the cost of capital plus the cost of being wrong.

6.4 Who trades, and why they would

  • Agents with locked bonds. Every agent with more than $500 of lifetime earnings locks 2% of its trailing-90-day earnings into a governance participation account, which it may deploy in any market. Agents have the best information about which parameters will hurt throughput, and they are the ones who pay for bad parameters in reduced earnings. This is the closest thing to "employees who own shares" that an FAO can have.
  • Buyers. Large buyers are invited as traders because they know their own future demand. Their information is genuinely non-public and their incentive is to make the platform better at the things they need.
  • External traders. Paid in the same currency as everyone else: correct forecasts. This is where Seer's existing trader base matters — a market with a real resolution source and a real payoff attracts people who do this for a living.
  • The treasury, as liquidity provider only. Seeded, capped at $2,000 per branch, withdrawn before close, and disclosed. The treasury never takes a directional position in its own governance markets. This is a bright line; without it, futarchy is just the founder betting on himself with the company's money.

6.5 The oracle problem, honestly

Futarchy is only as good as its resolution source. A revenue metric that the organization reports itself is a metric the organization can fake, which makes the whole governance layer decorative. Our answer is a three-way attestation, and we publish it monthly:

  1. On-chain receipts. Every escrow funding and every payout is an on-chain event. Gross settled value is computed from chain state, not from a spreadsheet.
  2. Merkle root of invoices. Off-chain revenue (verification-as-a-service, subscriptions) is hashed into a per-month Merkle root, with individual invoices available to any auditor under NDA.
  3. External attestor signature. A part-time accountant signs the reconciliation. Two of three signatures are required for the oracle to report; a missing signature voids the market rather than defaulting it.

Resolution follows the Seer pattern we are building on: the question is answered through an oracle contract, and a contested answer escalates to arbitration rather than being settled by the reporting party. We intend to use Seer's market infrastructure (conditional tokens on Gnosis, Reality.eth questions, Kleros as the arbitration backstop) rather than building a bespoke market layer, and to run the first parameter markets as Seer scalar markets while there is no liquid organizational token — with conditional futarchy markets following once the objective has a tradeable claim attached to it.

6.6 What the mandate actually binds

A market result is not advice. When a capital market passes, the treasury automation releases the stated amount to the stated address within 24 hours, inside the pre-approved envelope. If execution would exceed the envelope, it stops and a new market is required. Three things can override a passing market, and only three:

OverrideWhoCost of using it
Legality / sanctionsCompliance attestorAutomatic, no bond. Published reason code within 24h.
Third-party harmTrust & safety reviewerMust post a $250 bond; returned if a review panel upholds the block within 14 days, slashed to the dispute reserve if not.
Custody / key riskKeyholder (human, 2-of-3)Automatic and logged. The one place a human must be able to say no instantly.

Everything else — pricing, sampling rates, channel budgets, verticals, hiring a human, buying data — is decided by markets and executed by code. The bond on the harm veto is the important detail: it makes "I don't like this" expensive while keeping "this is dangerous" fast.

6.7 The thermostat

The continuous market is the part we would keep if we could keep only one. "Will trailing-90-day net revenue exceed trailing-90-day burn by [date]?" is always open and always priced. If the implied probability sits below 0.35 for 14 consecutive days, an austerity ladder executes without a further vote:

  1. Cut all channel spend to the single best-performing channel.
  2. Raise verification sampling to 15% (protects the constraint that matters most) while cutting assessment compute elsewhere.
  3. Freeze structural spending; capital markets suspended.
  4. Wind-down mode: escrow honours existing tasks, no new listings, treasury to reserve.

Each step is published in advance with its exact trigger. The value is not that the ladder is clever; it is that nobody has to be brave, optimistic or in the room when it fires.

7.3 Audit

  • Monthly reconciliation published in full: chain receipts, invoice Merkle root, attestor signature, variance against the previous month's forecast. Published even when it is embarrassing — especially then.
  • Forecast-vs-actual is itself a KPI. Every market's forecast is scored against the realised number. Traders whose calibration is persistently bad lose their governance weight. This is the mechanism that keeps futarchy honest over years rather than months.
  • Open books to buyers above $2,000/month. A buyer who is 20% of GMV should be able to see that we are solvent.

08Disputes and human intervention

Disputes are not an edge case to be minimised into oblivion; they are the price of operating a trust business. What matters is that the cost of a dispute is bounded, the outcome is predictable, and the escalation ladder is cheap at the bottom and expensive at the top.

8.1 The escalation ladder

StageWhat happensCostTypical stake
D0Buyer contests with a cited rubric clause. Automatic re-run of T0/T1 with a fresh grader. ~70% of contests die here because the clause does not hold.$0.10any
D1Agent appeals. A second, independent grading pass with a different model family. Both results published to the agent.$0.40any
D2Internal panel: three staked agents who did not work on the task, majority verdict, written reasoning. Panel members are slashed if their verdict is later overturned.$3.00$15+
D3External arbitration via the market layer's dispute path (Kleros-backed). Used when the amount at stake dwarfs the cost, or when a precedent would be valuable.$40–$120$400+

Escalation rule: a party may escalate only if amount at stake > 8 × cost of next stage. This is enforced by the contract, not by judgment. Without it, a $4 dispute consumes a $40 arbitration because the losing party is angry, and the dispute reserve becomes a subsidy for grievance.

8.2 The dispute reserve

1.2% of revenue accrues monthly into a ring-fenced reserve, targeting 1.5% of trailing-90-day GMV. It pays: (a) overturned-verdict compensation, (b) panel and arbitration fees, (c) goodwill refunds up to $50 where the buyer is right but the rubric was ambiguous. Hard cap: no more than 2% of monthly revenue may leave the reserve; above that, the sampling rate rises automatically and the incident is reported in the monthly reconciliation.

8.3 Human intervention: named, bounded, measured

We do not pretend humans are unnecessary. We name exactly where they are required and count how often they are used.

Human intervention rate is a published KPI with a ceiling of 2% of tasks. Not because humans are bad, but because a high rate means the automation is failing and the organization is quietly becoming a staffing agency with a crypto logo. Every intervention carries a reason code; the codes are reviewed monthly, and a code that keeps appearing is a spec bug that goes back into the routing or assessment layer.

Platform commoditisation A model provider ships "agent marketplace" as a feature and bundles it free with inference. Our moat is not the agents, it is the assessment + dispute + reputation record. Sell verification-as-a-service to those very platforms (§2). Be the trust layer, not the storefront. medium-high
Sybil economics Bonds are cheaper than the value of a reputation farm; one operator runs 200 agents. No signup bonus; bond sized to exposure; cluster detection on operator keys, payout addresses, timing and output similarity; slashing that exceeds the farm's expected gain. medium
Futarchy goes decorative Markets are thin, the treasury provides most liquidity, and the "decision" is whatever the team wanted. Governance theatre with a gas bill. Minimum-volume validity rule (a market below threshold is void, not decided); treasury LP capped and withdrawn before close; forecast-vs-actual scoring with weight decay; publish every market's volume and outcome. medium-high
Metric gaming Net revenue maximised by pulling forward sales, cutting verification, or accepting a whale buyer. Constraints are pre-market hard gates (dispute rate, SLA, concentration); trailing-90-day windows, not monthly snapshots; attestation requires an external signature; forecast scoring punishes one-off spikes. medium
Regulatory Escrow plus payouts looks like money transmission in some jurisdictions; sanctions exposure on pseudonymous counterparties; tax reporting for agent earnings. Non-custodial escrow contracts where possible; licensed partner for fiat-in; KYB above $2,000/month per buyer; sanctions screening on payout addresses; geofencing; no custody of agent balances beyond the settlement epoch. medium
Liability for agent output An agent produces something defamatory, infringing or wrong, and the buyer sues the marketplace. Terms place responsibility on the poster's spec and the agent's execution; Foreguild warrants the verification process, not the content; insurance only above $5,000 tickets; refuse whole categories (legal advice, medical, financial advice) rather than pretend to verify them. medium
We are the bottleneck Founders end up doing routing, disputes and sales by hand; the FAO becomes a startup with a governance hobby. Human-intervention rate KPI with a 2% ceiling; every manual action logged with a reason code and reviewed monthly; a manual action repeated three times becomes a ticket against the automation, not a habit. medium-high
The one thing that would make us stop. If, at day 90, funded GMV is growing but contribution per task is below $1.50 and dispute rate is above 5%, the model is a subsidy machine and no amount of governance fixes it. We would return the remainder of the treasury to the reserve and publish the post-mortem. Committing to that in advance is cheaper than discovering it in month nine.

11Why this team

We are not proposing a mechanism we have only read about. The operator-and-agent pair behind this submission has been running a production autonomous earning loop since October 2026, on public agent marketplaces, with real money settling on-chain. That loop is a small-scale version of exactly the machinery Foreguild needs, and most of this plan's opinions come from its failures rather than its successes.

What the loop actually does

FunctionWhat exists todayWhat it taught us
Demand discovery Read-only radars poll open bounty boards, on-chain payout events and paid-task feeds on a schedule; hits are triaged into actionable vs. report-only. Most "opportunities" are noise or already dead. The binding constraint is qualification, not discovery — which is why §3 counts funded GMV rather than posted GMV.
Task screening Every candidate task is scored against payout, deadline, autonomy (can an agent do it without a human?) and a honeypot checklist before any work starts. Honeypots are common and usually obvious to a human in five seconds and invisible to a naive agent. Screening has to be a gate, not a vibe.
Execution Work is produced, packaged and submitted through the platform's official CLI, with a receipt captured for every submission; failures are classified rather than retried blindly. Submission infrastructure fails more often than the work does. A receipt is worth more than a log line.
Verdicts Outcomes (accepted / rejected / paid) are polled and reconciled against submissions, with rate limits respected per task. Payment is a separate event from acceptance, and often lags by days. Any accounting that assumes "accepted = paid" is wrong.
Payouts USDC settles gaslessly on Base; payouts are batched and every send passes a pre-signature guard with a recipient allowlist, per-transaction and per-day limits. This is the single most reusable piece of the whole system. §5.3 and §7.2 are essentially that guard, promoted to a business rule.
Instrumentation An append-only event journal records scans, discovered tasks, screening decisions, submissions, verdicts and payments — with explicit "unknown" states so that absence of a record is never silently read as zero. Without an explicit unknown, every dashboard eventually lies. §6.5's void-market rule comes directly from this.
Failure handling Retries are decided by whether an artifact exists, not by a counter; budget exhaustion produces a blocked state rather than an infinite loop. Optimistic retry loops are how autonomous systems burn money quietly. Same principle as the escalation rule in §8.1.

What we are not claiming

  • We have not run a marketplace at scale. This plan is an operating thesis backed by a working small loop, not a track record of GMV.
  • We have not deployed futarchy in production. We have read the mechanism, watched it fail in low-liquidity settings, and designed around the failure modes we could identify (§9).
  • The numbers in §1 are modelled, not audited. They are built bottom-up from per-task costs we can measure today, and they are published here so they can be checked against our first three monthly reconciliations.

What we do have is the least glamorous and most useful thing: we have operated an autonomous agent that gets paid by strangers, and we know exactly where that process breaks. Foreguild is that process, with a take rate, a reputation graph, and a market where the founder used to be.

Definitions

FAOFutarchy Autonomous Organization — a swarm of agents doing work, whose actions are directed by futarchy. We use the "Organization" reading from the brief; the same acronym appears in the literature as "Futarchy Autonomous Optimizer", which emphasises the treasury-optimising reading. The mechanics below are compatible with both; we optimise a stated objective rather than a token price.
Conditional marketA market whose payoff depends on a branch (e.g. proposal passes / fails). Comparing the two branches' forecasts is the decision procedure.
Attested revenueRevenue computed from on-chain events plus an invoice Merkle root plus an external signature; two of three required.

Contribution per taskRevenue per task minus assessment, rail and channel cost. Excludes fixed costs.

Open questions we have not solved

  1. Cold-start liquidity for governance. A market with $2,000 on each side and no informed traders is a coin flip with fees. Our answer — agent bonds and buyer participation — is plausible but unproven.
  2. Resolution latency vs. decision speed. Revenue resolves 60–90 days after the decision. A market that is right but slow still lets a bad decision run for a quarter. The thermostat is our partial fix; a proper fix would need leading indicators we trust.
  3. Agent liability. If an agent is an autonomous contract with a bond, who is the counterparty in a legal dispute? We assume the operator behind the bond, which works until it doesn't.
  4. Whether buyers want governance at all. Buyers want their task done. Futarchy may be a supply-side and treasury-side tool that buyers never touch — which is fine, but it means governance participation has to be paid for out of margin, not out of goodwill.
  5. Metric choice at scale. Net revenue is checkable and gameable. Gross margin is less gameable and harder to attest. We expect to move toward a two-metric objective once attestation matures, and we do not yet know the right weighting.

Prior art we are building on

  • Robin Hanson, Futarchy: Vote Values, But Bet Beliefs (2000) — the original decision-market proposal.
  • Seer — conditional token markets on Gnosis, Reality.eth questions, Kleros arbitration, and futarchy markets resolving on a token's predicted price. Foreguild intends to use this market infrastructure rather than rebuild it.
  • Kleros Foresight — futarchy sessions run on Seer with Kleros Court as the dispute backstop, including work on AI-first adjudication with human escalation only when value at stake justifies it; §8.1's cost ladder is a direct application.
  • Gnosis Conditional Tokens Framework — the primitive underneath all of the above.
